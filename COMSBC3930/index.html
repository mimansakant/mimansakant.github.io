Manhattan Skyline Soldered

NYC skyline sculpture created using a protoboard, a 12 pin header, a brass rod, a soldering iron, and lead solder.

← Scroll to view all images →

Reflection

It was really cool learning how to solder for the first time, and it ended up being more intuitive than I expected. Most of my previous hands-on work has been more mechanical and force-based, like drilling, so working delicately with small pins and tiny holes felt very different.

I also enjoyed how open-ended the project felt. Even with relatively simple components, there were a lot of creative possibilities. This made me interested in exploring other contexts where soldering is used in more artistic ways.

Flashing Jet PCB

Custom jet-shaped PCB implementing a 555 timer astable circuit. A yellow LED represents a flame coming from the bottom of the jet, powered by a CR2032 coin cell battery.

← Scroll to view all images →

Overview

This project involved designing and routing a 555 timer LED flasher circuit in KiCad, milling the PCB on a Carvey CNC machine, and soldering all through-hole components including the timer IC, resistors, ceramic capacitors, LED, and battery holder. The blinking LED simulates a jet engine flame, bringing motion and narrative into an otherwise functional circuit.

A detailed breakdown of the design process, fabrication workflow, and circuit behavior can be found in the full project documentation.

Rainfall

A generative poetry installation running on the ESP32 TTGO T-Display. Ten hand-written poems — themed around nature and impermanence — fall like rain from drifting pixel clouds and disappear before they can be read twice.

More images coming soon

Overview

This project runs on the ESP32 TTGO T-Display and generates an animated rain scene with poems falling down the screen. Poems generated by word banks are randomly assigned to drops that fall at different speeds and positions, so the composition is always changing. It was installed in a hallway for the class exhibition.

I wanted the text to feel like it was part of the rain rather than just overlaid on it, so the poem lines fall alongside the raindrops and disappear off the bottom before finishing, which ended up working really well in a space where people are just passing through anyway.

Radio Building

A touch-sensitive architectural sculpture that tunes into live internet radio stations. Five copper touch points — embedded in the facade of a 3D printed art deco building — each trigger a different stream, while a double touch switches between two curated mood modes.

← Scroll to view all images →

Overview

This radio lets you tune into the city's frequencies by touching it. The structure is a 3D printed tabletop radio.

Five copper tape touch points are inlaid flush into different parts of the radio. Each is wired through channels inside the walls down to an ESP32 in the hollow base, which reads the capacitive touch pins and sends the signal over serial to a laptop running a Python audio script. The speaker is hidden behind a 3D printed lattice grille on the front of the radio.

Meownet — A Self-Balancing Pachinko Cat

A runtime-monitored pachinko machine that watches where the balls fall and physically intervenes when the distribution gets too lopsided. Built by a team of five over two and a half weeks using three ESP32 nodes communicating over ESP-NOW, with stepper-driven deflection arms and an animated cat eye on a TTGO T-Display.

← Scroll to view all images →

Overview

The board is a hand-painted black cat with brass nails as the pachinko field and a 3D-printed five-channel ball collector at the bottom. Three slots are "food," two are "poison," and two stepper-driven arms partway down the playfield swing to deflect balls away from over-represented poison channels — Meownet's job is to keep herself alive.

Detection uses copper tape contact switches: two strips per channel with a small gap, bridged by the metal ball as it rolls through. A runtime monitor written in RTLola watches the distribution and only triggers broadcasts at meaningful inflection points, rather than flooding ESP-NOW on every ball drop. Arm behavior is proportional — the more skewed a channel becomes, the further its corresponding arm tilts.

The trickiest lesson was pin budgeting on the ESP32. We lost an afternoon to the input-only pins (34, 35, 36, 39) silently failing on digitalWrite with no error — splitting the work across three boards wasn't just architectural, it was a hardware necessity.

Mini Golf

An interactive embedded mini golf system built around multiple ESP32 microcontrollers communicating wirelessly via ESP-NOW. Sensors detect when the ball is sunk, triggering a celebration animation on a TFT display and an audio fanfare, while a stepper-motor-driven lighthouse door oscillates as a moving obstacle. Final project for COMS3930, built with Kristy Martinez and Victoria Cao.

← Scroll to view all images →

Overview

Three ESP32 boards handle different responsibilities: a Ball Sensor ESP32 reads two IR/photoresistor inputs at the lighthouse hole and broadcasts a trigger over ESP-NOW; a Congrats Display ESP32 (TTGO T-Display) receives that trigger and renders a scrolling "CONGRATULATIONS!!!!" animation; and a third Motor ESP32 runs standalone, driving a stepper through half-step sequencing to swing the lighthouse door back and forth as a moving obstacle.

A Python script on a connected laptop listens on serial for ball detection events and plays a celebration .wav via afplay, bridging the embedded system to the host audio. Rendering on the TFT goes through an off-screen sprite to eliminate flicker, and ESP-NOW receive callbacks set flags that are checked in the main loop to avoid ISR re-entrancy issues.
